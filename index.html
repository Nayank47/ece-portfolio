01 / Biography

About me

I’m interested in the point where software meets the physical world. On the RoboNav electrical team at RoboJackets, I’m getting experience with that intersection by working on a rover with other students. My interests also extend to applied AI, from my internship at Huduku AI to research on how AI could be used in Formula 1.

Teaching and mentoring

As a lead coding instructor at Code Ninjas, I guided hands-on programming projects for more than 50 students and trained five new instructors. Debugging was a recurring part of the job. I’d walk through what a student expected their program to do and help them find where it behaved differently. Training instructors also gave me practice explaining the same material to people with different levels of experience.

Making software easier to inspect

During my internship at Huduku AI, I added clickable citations so readers could check the sources behind AI-generated answers. I also integrated Langfuse so the team could review requests and responses and monitor performance. I helped develop an AI-generated newsletter about potential cybersecurity threats. These projects showed me how much the usefulness of an AI tool depends on whether people can check its output and see how it behaves.

Technical skills

I use Python and Java for programming and Git and GitHub for version control. My hardware experience includes ESP32, breadboarding, and sensor integration. I use SolidWorks for mechanical design, including the drive assembly for my rain cover project.

02 / Technical work

Projects

RoboJackets · In progress

RoboNav Rover

I’m a member of the electrical team on RoboNav, where I’m working with other RoboJackets members on a rover. The project gives me a way to explore electrical design in a team setting and see how the electrical side fits alongside mechanical hardware and software.

One question I’m interested in is how the team moves from individual parts to a system it can test. A rover brings together sensing, control, and motion. Understanding the interfaces between those pieces is part of learning to troubleshoot the system as a whole.

Discovery Project Feature · Breadboard stage

Automatic Dispenser with a Voice Sensor

My Discovery Project explores how a voice sensor could trigger an automatic dispenser. I’ve placed the voice sensor on a breadboard as the starting point for the prototype.

The problem

The design question is how to turn input from a voice sensor into a dispensing action. The first step is to understand what output the sensor provides when it picks up my voice.

The process

I’m starting with the sensor on the breadboard. The next step is to check its output under different conditions, such as quiet, speech, and background noise. That would help me decide what signal the dispenser should use as a trigger.

Current progress

The breadboard setup is the current milestone. Once I’ve tested the sensor, I can use those observations to plan how to connect it to the dispensing mechanism.

Research

AI Applications in Formula 1

I’ve researched how AI could be applied in Formula 1. The topic connects my interest in racing with a technical question about how to use data when decisions have to be made quickly. I’m interested in where tools could help people interpret information and how teams could evaluate the quality of that help.

Race strategy is one example of what makes the topic interesting to me. A useful analysis has to account for changing conditions and uncertainty, and a team has to be able to act on the result. That makes the problem as much about how a recommendation is used as how it is generated.

CREATE-X · Design in progress

Automatic Tennis-Court Rain Cover

I’m designing a system that protects an outdoor tennis court by automatically deploying and retracting a weather-resistant cover. Georgia Tech’s CREATE-X Ideas to Prototype program selected the project and provides mentorship and resources for prototype development.

My current work focuses on the mechanism that moves the cover. I’m designing the motor, roller, gear, and bearing assembly in SolidWorks for the first functional prototype.

05 / ECE Roadmap

Career goals

My long-term goal is to work on robotic and automated systems that combine software, electronics, and moving hardware. I’m especially interested in embedded systems because the decisions made in code have immediate physical consequences. A sensor reading can determine whether a motor moves, and the mechanical design affects whether that movement does what the system needs it to do. I want to be able to follow a problem across those boundaries and explain where it starts.

Build a foundation at Georgia Tech

My near-term priority is to strengthen my understanding of circuits, programming, and digital systems, then apply those ideas on the bench. A practical goal is to take an input from a sensor, observe it under different conditions, and use it to control an output. I can show progress by documenting the wiring, saving the code, and recording how the system responds when I change the input. That gives me a concrete way to connect coursework to the dispenser project.

Develop and test physical systems

Before graduation, I want to build on my RoboNav experience and pursue an internship or research opportunity that involves developing and testing physical systems. On a rover, each part has to work in the context of what the rest of the team is building. I want to get better at explaining what I’ve tested, documenting how to reproduce it, and identifying which part of a system to investigate when something fails.

Grow into hardware and software integration

After graduation, I want to contribute to a team building robots or automation equipment. I’d like to start by taking responsibility for a subsystem, such as a sensor or motor controller, and learn how it fits into the larger product. Over time, I want to take on more of the integration work, from deciding how parts communicate to defining what the team needs to measure before the system is ready for use.
